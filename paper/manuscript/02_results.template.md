# Results

## Layers couple reaction kinetics to growth and division

Biomodelling.jl 2.0 is organised in layers (Fig. 1). A model is a list of reactions with mass-action, Hill or custom kinetics and named parameters; promoter states are explicit species, grouped so that they are inherited at division and doubled at replication. Four kernels advance a cell's state at fixed volume: the exact direct method, fixed-step tau-leaping, a hybrid scheme that falls back to the exact method whenever a leap would produce a negative count, and adaptive tau-leaping with critical-reaction handling. The population layer grows each cell's volume exponentially, scales propensities with volume so that concentrations are invariant, replicates genes at a set point of the cycle, divides cells by a sizer, adder or timer rule with binomial or over-dispersed partitioning, and controls the population size by Moran-like replacement, free growth or a logistic hazard. A perturbation layer applies dose schedules, state-dependent death, growth inhibition, drug-induced rate changes and genetic perturbations. Every cell's ancestry, fate and state at birth and division enter a lineage table, from which heritability, autocorrelation, memory timescales, fluctuation-test and clonal-memory statistics follow. Observation models convert true counts into scRNA-seq, smFISH or time-lapse measurements, and the same forward model drives likelihood-free inference. Table 1 sets these capabilities against those of the simulators most used to benchmark single-cell methods.

## The engine matches exact laws and an independent package

We first verified the kernels against exact stationary laws (Fig. 2). For a birth-death process the simulated distribution of 50,000 cells is indistinguishable from the Poisson law (Fig. 2a); for the telegraph model in an intermediate and in a bursty regime it matches the Beta-Poisson distribution [@peccoud1995] (Fig. 2b); and for bursty protein production it matches the negative binomial (Fig. 2c). Across kernels, Kolmogorov-Smirnov distances to the exact law with 20,000 cells lie below the 99% critical value for the direct method, for the adaptive scheme at both tolerances and for the hybrid scheme at the shorter step, and above it for the hybrid scheme at the longer step ({{ks_hybrid_coarse}} against a critical value of {{ks_crit20k}}; Fig. 2d). That is the expected behaviour of an approximate kernel, and it sets the step below which the approximation is not detectable at this sample size; wall-clock costs are {{fig2d_times}} per 10⁴ cells. Simulating the same telegraph model with JumpProcesses.jl gives distributions whose two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic against Biomodelling.jl is {{ks_two}} (99% critical value {{ks_crit}}; Fig. 2e), with a {{speed_ratio}} difference in serial wall-clock time. Those tests exercise one cell at fixed volume. The layer above them, growth, replication, partitioning and the difference between a lineage and a population, has exact solutions of its own for this class of model, which take different closed forms in the two settings [@beentjes2020; @jia2023; @thomas2017]. We reproduced four analytically solvable members of that class, with constitutive, bursty and two-state promoter kinetics under a memoryless interdivision time and with volume-scaled synthesis from a replicating gene under a deterministic cycle, in both modes. {{exact_result}} (Supplementary Note 14 and Supplementary Fig. 6). Population simulations scale linearly with the number of cells: {{runtime_10k}} for {{runtime_case}} with four threads (Fig. 2f and Supplementary Table 3). Scaling in the number of reactions depends on the kernel and is close to linear only for the hybrid one ({{runtime_scaling}}), because the direct method draws one event at a time while the hybrid kernel leaps over the non-critical channels in a fixed number of operations.

## Physiology generates memory, scaling and cycle effects

The physiology of the population layer suffices to produce properties of single-cell data that phenomenological simulators must impose by hand (Fig. 3). In a single lineage of a slowly switching gene, mRNA and protein track volume within a cycle and are halved at division, while the promoter state, inherited by both daughters, persists over many divisions (Fig. 3a). For a fast-switching gene without replication, transcript numbers scale linearly with cell volume, so that concentration is homeostatic; adding mid-cycle gene replication doubles transcription in the second half of the cycle and lifts counts of large cells above the volume-scaling line (Fig. 3b), the copy-number effect that mechanistic fits to scRNA-seq data must account for [@sukys2025]. Correlations of protein concentration at division between mothers and daughters, sisters and cousins fall from {{md_slow}} to {{md_fast}} as the promoter switching rate crosses the inverse cell-cycle time (Fig. 3c), mother-daughter and sister pairs correlating similarly at slow switching and mother-daughter pairs more strongly as switching quickens, cousins least, and mRNA less heritable than protein. The memory timescale extracted from the generation-resolved lineage autocorrelation is set by the promoter switching time and is nearly independent of the cell-cycle time as long as memory outlasts the cycle; when the two are comparable the estimate is bounded below by one cell cycle, the resolution of the autocorrelation (Fig. 3d). As reported for real lineages [@zhang2025], the coefficient of variation of a stable protein's concentration is larger across the population than along single lineages, whether or not cell-cycle times are noisy, and over-dispersed partitioning adds variability in both perspectives (Fig. 3e). Telegraph parameters fitted separately to cells before and after replication move in the direction gene replication implies, but not by the factor it alone would give (Fig. 3f). Between the one-copy and the two-copy subsets the fitted transcription rate rises {{ktx_fold}}-fold, of which {{vol_fold}}-fold is the larger volume of cells that have already replicated, leaving {{ktx_per_vol_fold}}-fold per unit volume rather than the twofold that doubling the copy number implies; the fitted activation rate rises {{kon_fold}}-fold over the same comparison, and the pooled fit that ignores the cell cycle returns a transcription rate above both subsets. Part of that shortfall is misspecification rather than copy number, and the figure shows it: two independently switching alleles are not one telegraph gene, and neither subset fit recovers the truth (0.5 and 1.5 for the switching rates and 2 for the transcription rate as Fig. 3f scales it, that is the true 40 transcripts per unit time per unit volume per allele divided by twenty, against {{fit_one_copy}} fitted to the one-copy cells and {{fit_pooled}} pooled, whose transcription rates are scaled the same way). What the panel demonstrates is that a cell-cycle-blind fit is biased, not how large the copy-number effect is.

## One resistance gene reproduces tolerance signatures

To test whether heritable expression states suffice to reproduce the lineage and memory signatures of drug-tolerant persistence, we coupled a resistance gene with slow promoter switching (memory timescale {{mem_time}} time units, cell-cycle time 20) to a death hazard that rises with dose and is suppressed by the concentration of the gene's protein product (Methods). Under continuous, pulsed and reduced-dose schedules the population first collapses as low-expressing cells die, then regrows from high-expressing survivors (Fig. 4a); kill curves are biphasic with a surviving fraction that falls with dose (Fig. 4b). Between doses 0.5 and 2 the net rate of population decline over the first 30 time units of treatment rises from {{decay_lo}} to {{decay_hi}} per time unit ({{decay_fold}}-fold), while the mean division time of cells born under drug changes by {{divtime_change}} and the mean time to death of killed cells moves only from {{deathtime_range}} time units (Fig. 4c; the single-cell timing distributions are in Supplementary Fig. 1). This reproduces the observation that population decay depends strongly on dose even though single-cell division and death times barely do [@iyer2025], because fate is biased by states that exist before the drug arrives. Unlike the growth-arrested persisters of the canonical definition [@sharma2010], the survivors here divide at an essentially unchanged rate (Fig. 4c, inset), so the model reproduces the heritable-state selection signatures of tolerance rather than its proliferative-arrest hallmark.

Related cells accordingly share the fate of their lineages. Among cells born in the 25 time units before the drug, one whose sister founded a surviving lineage does so itself with probability {{sis_cond}}, against a marginal probability of {{sis_marg}} ({{sis_fold}}-fold enrichment over {{sis_n}} sister pairs; {{cous_fold}}-fold for cousins), whereas in a fast-switching control, in which survival is rarer, the {{sis_fold_fast}}- and {{cous_fold_fast}}-fold values the panel prints rest on {{fast_pairs}} in which both lineages survived, and the concordance sits at the independent expectation ({{cyc4_fast_b}} above it, against {{cyc4_mem_b}} for the memory gene; Fig. 4d). When tolerance pre-exists, survivors descend from a minority of founder clones and the effective number of clones drops from {{clones_before}} to {{clones_after_pre}}; when tolerance is drug-induced, so that the drug raises the promoter activation rate of every cell, barcode diversity is largely preserved ({{clones_after_ind}}; Fig. 4e). This is the signature that distinguishes the two origins in barcoding experiments [@shaffer2017; @iyer2025]; the follow-up method that traces resistant fates back to their drug-naive precursors puts those precursors at an initial frequency of about 1 in 1,000 to 1 in 10,000 cells [@emert2021]. The melanoma case study below does not sit in that regime: it uses 1:200, the per-marker frequency at which rare cells express resistance genes before drug [@shaffer2017], which is 5- to 50-fold higher. That range is in any case not resolvable at the size of this study, since a draw of its 2,000 founders would contain no pre-resistant cell at all with probability 0.8 at 1 in 10,000. The consequence is a larger and less clonal pre-existing resistant pool, so that among the arms which do lose control it is lost earlier than the traced frequency would give; the ranking of the schedules follows from the growth of resistant cells with and without drug rather than from their initial frequency, but it was not re-tested at a lower fraction.

Disrupting expression memory changes the outcome, and when it is disrupted matters. Accelerating promoter switching 20-fold from two cell-cycle times before the drug does not reduce the number of surviving clones if the acceleration stops when the drug arrives: it raises them, from {{clones_none}} to {{clones_predrug}} ({{clones_predrug_p}}). Reshuffling spreads the resistant state over more lineages and moves the resistant fraction towards its stationary value ({{resfrac_none}} to {{resfrac_predrug}}), so more clones carry a protected cell when the drug arrives, even though protection itself is untouched. Maintaining it during exposure reduces the count to {{clones_during}} clones ({{cells_during}} cells, against {{cells_none}}; Fig. 4f), because resistant cells now leave the protected state within a fraction of the time they need to found a colony. In the model, memory must be disrupted while the drug is present.

Two published experiments bear on this prediction and they point in opposite directions, so it is worth being explicit about which perturbation each one performs. A CRISPR screen for modulators of single-cell variability found that inhibiting DOT1L *before* adding a BRAF inhibitor produced more therapy resistance than giving the two together [@torre2021], which is the ordering the model predicts. A lineage-tracing study that identified PI3K and TGF-β signalling as switching modulators found the opposite for its own pretreatment: a PI3K inhibitor applied before targeted therapy left less resistance than targeted therapy alone [@harmange2023]. The two results are not in conflict with each other, and only the first is a test of what is simulated here. The perturbation in the model is symmetric: it multiplies the activation and the inactivation rate by the same factor, so it accelerates relaxation towards the stationary resistant fraction without moving that fraction, and its only effect before the drug is to spread the resistant state over more lineages. A modulator that shifts the balance between the states is a different perturbation, and a pretreatment that drives cells out of the primed state lowers the number of protected cells at the moment the drug arrives, which reduces resistance whether or not it is maintained. The prediction here is therefore specific to perturbations that speed switching without biasing it, of which DOT1L inhibition appears to be one and PI3K inhibition is not; distinguishing the two experimentally requires measuring the resistant fraction as well as the switching rate, which memory-sequencing and lineage-tracing assays do. Compartment theory for phenotypic switching already predicts that an agent which perturbs the retention of the resistant state can turn treatment failure into success [@gunnarsson2020]; what the simulations add is the timing requirement and a lineage-resolved readout, the number of surviving clones, by which it can be tested. A transient pulse enriches high-expressing cells and, with slow switching, the enrichment persists for many generations after the drug is removed, whereas with fast switching it relaxes within a few cycles (Fig. 4g): phenotypic selection with stable inheritance, as in the original MGMT model [@lasri2020]. Finally, scanning release periods and doses of intermittent schedules (Fig. 4h), the most effective schedule is {{best_pre}} for pre-existing tolerance, {{best_cost}} when the resistant state carries a fitness cost, and {{best_ind}} for drug-induced tolerance. {{sched_seed_note}} Without a fitness cost nothing beats continuous dosing at the highest dose, since every release period lets the resistant subpopulation regrow (the drug does not act on the switching rates in this model; when it also stabilises the resistant state, as in the melanoma case study below, holidays regain a small advantage even without a cost). With a fitness cost the population declines under every schedule at dose ≥ 0.5, and release periods of up to 10 time units match continuous dosing ({{cont_cost}} per time unit under continuous dose 2), because cells that leave the resistant state during a holiday are killed on re-exposure. For drug-induced tolerance the ranking of doses reverses: the highest dose induces tolerance in more cells than it kills, so dose 1 outperforms dose 2 at every release period ({{best_ind}}, against {{cont_ind}} for continuous dose 2), the intermediate-dose optimum reported previously [@corigliano2025]; the benefit of release periods reported in that work does not appear here, because in this model the induced state decays at the same rate with and without drug, so a holiday only postpones the death of reverting cells while all cells grow.

The hazard above does not depend on where a cell is in its division cycle, and the calibration below will show that fate data cannot exclude one that does, so we repeated these simulations with three quarters of the hazard confined to a window around mid-cycle. {{cycle_case_result}} (Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary Fig. 5).

## Simulated ground truth benchmarks inference methods

Because every cell's clone, lineage and true state are known, the framework supplies ground truth that observational data cannot (Fig. 5). For 40 independent genes whose memory times span three decades, MemorySeq-style clonal variance scores computed on true concentrations rise from {{score_min}} for genes with memory far below the cell-cycle time to a mean of {{score_max}} for genes with memory beyond a hundred time units, five cell-cycle times, the highest single gene reaching {{score_peak}} ({{score_peak_seq}} on sequenced counts), and the ranking survives computation on sequenced counts at 20% capture efficiency (Fig. 5a); the score separates genes with memory longer than the cycle from the rest with an area under the receiver operating characteristic (ROC) curve of {{auc_true}} on true concentrations and {{auc_seq}} on sequenced counts. For a 30-gene network, small enough that the methods were run with their default settings and the ranking of datasets rather than the absolute accuracy is what should be read, simulated at fixed volume, in a dividing population and after sequencing, correlation-based and GENIE3 inference lose most of their accuracy in the dividing population when raw counts are used (area under the precision-recall curve {{aupr_pearson_fixed}} to {{aupr_pearson_counts}} for Pearson correlation and {{aupr_genie3_fixed}} to {{aupr_genie3_counts}} for GENIE3, against {{aupr_random_undir}} for a random ranking of undirected pairs and {{aupr_random_dir}} for directed edges), because volume and gene copy number induce positive correlations between all genes. Normalising counts to concentrations removes the volume effect but recovers little ({{aupr_pearson_conc}} and {{aupr_genie3_conc}}), since the dependence on copy number and cell age remains; regressing expression on the true cell-cycle covariates, which the simulation provides and which cell-cycle scoring approximates in real data, recovers most of the loss ({{aupr_pearson_reg}} and {{aupr_genie3_reg}}). Sequencing at 15% capture efficiency lowers accuracy further ({{aupr_pearson_seq}} raw and {{aupr_pearson_norm}} library-size-normalised for Pearson correlation; {{aupr_genie3_seq}} and {{aupr_genie3_norm}} for GENIE3; Fig. 5b). One comparison in that panel runs the wrong way: for Pearson correlation the library-size-normalised sequenced counts score above the true concentrations ({{aupr_pearson_norm}} against {{aupr_pearson_conc}}). Dividing counts by the library size removes cell-to-cell scale, and in a dividing population that scale is dominated by volume and gene copy number, so the normalisation acts as a coarse correction for the growth confound that dividing by the true volume does not make. It is not evidence that sequencing helps; the fixed-volume ceiling ({{aupr_pearson_fixed}}) is above both, and the cell-cycle regression ({{aupr_pearson_reg}}) is above both as well. Imputing the sequenced counts before inference recovers part of that loss: MAGIC raises the area under the precision-recall curve from {{aupr_pearson_seq}} to {{aupr_p_magic}} for Pearson correlation and from {{aupr_genie3_seq}} to {{aupr_g_magic}} for GENIE3, and kNN-smoothing gives {{aupr_p_knn}} and {{aupr_g_knn}} (Fig. 5c). Neither reaches the accuracy obtained at fixed volume, nor that of regressing out the cell-cycle covariates, so imputation mitigates the sequencing loss without touching the growth confound that produces most of it [@lasri2022]. Knocking down the five most connected regulators of a 20-gene network, again small and run at default settings, gives true fold changes that a no-change baseline predicts with a mean coefficient of determination of {{r2_zero}}. Propagating observational correlations beats that baseline for {{r2_corr_nwin}} knockdowns, with coefficients of determination from {{r2_corr_good}}, and fails on the remaining one ({{r2_corr_worst}}), so its mean over the five is {{r2_corr}} against a median of {{r2_corr_med}} (Fig. 5d). A predictor that is useful for most perturbations and badly wrong for one is precisely what an out-of-distribution evaluation has to expose, and exposing it needs the causal ground truth that the simulation supplies [@ahlmanneltze2025; @vollenweider2026].

## Inference recovers parameters and exposes division bias

On a snapshot of 2,000 non-dividing cells, ABC-SMC with log-uniform priors over three decades and the Wasserstein distance between simulated and observed counts [@bernton2019] concentrates on the true telegraph parameters and agrees with the exact maximum-likelihood estimate (posterior medians {{abc_a}} for truth 0.3, 0.6, 20; Fig. 6a), the broadest posterior falling on the promoter inactivation rate, which a single snapshot constrains least. On a snapshot of a growing and dividing population with volume-scaled transcription and mid-cycle replication, the naive telegraph fit is biased ({{naive_b}}) because volume and copy number inflate counts and their dispersion, whereas ABC with the population simulator as forward model recovers the truth ({{abc_b}}; Fig. 6b). Drug parameters follow from population data in the same way: from a kill curve and the concordance of sister fates, the posterior over the maximal death rate and the protection threshold is a ridge (weighted correlation of the two log parameters {{ridge_corr}}) that contains the truth (Fig. 6c), and posterior predictive kill curves simulated at the posterior median reproduce the observed one (Fig. 6d). The two summaries constrain the product of death rate and unprotected fraction, so separating the parameters requires measuring the protective protein: an experimental-design question the forward model answers before an experiment is run (the tolerance and acceptance-rate schedules of all three ABC runs are in Supplementary Fig. 2).

## Calibration to time-lapse data is tested on held-out data

A simulator is useful for treatment questions only if it can be fitted to the data a laboratory produces and then predict what it was not fitted to. We therefore calibrated the resistance-gene model to time-lapse measurements in which U2OS cells were tracked for three days under 7, 10 and 13 µM cisplatin and every cell present at drug addition was scored as dying, dividing or surviving without dividing [@iyer2025] (Supplementary Table 4). Six parameters, the two promoter switching rates, the maximal death rate, the EC$_{50}$ and Hill coefficient of death and the IC$_{50}$ of growth arrest, were fitted to the fate fractions at 7 and 13 µM by simulated moments with common random numbers, under a weak prior keeping the memory of the resistant state near the two to three generations that the same study inferred from lineage correlations (Methods). The calibrated model reproduces the training fractions with a root-mean-square error of {{rmse_train}} (memory {{cal_memgen}} generations, resistant fraction {{cal_pon}}, EC$_{50}$ {{cal_ec50}} µM; Fig. 7a,f). Three related numbers appear in this Article and are not interchangeable. Every root-mean-square error quoted in the text is the residual of the seed-averaged fate fractions pooled over every fraction of the concentrations named: four seeds per condition for the calibration, here and at the held-out concentration, and three for the comparison of the two killing mechanisms below, whose training errors alone are single-seed, because the optimiser evaluates one seed per candidate. The colour scale of Fig. 7e is the quantity the optimiser minimised, which is evaluated on a single seed and adds the memory prior: at the optimum it is {{cal_objective}}, made up of {{cal_objective_parts}}. The comparison of the two killing mechanisms in Supplementary Note 12 uses that single-seed training error for both models, so that they are scored by the same rule as the fit itself. That error should be read against the precision of the measurement: the observed fractions carry a mean binomial standard error of {{binom_se}} and repeated simulations differ by {{sim_se}}, so the fit is about {{rmse_train_in_se}} times the combined noise and the model does not reproduce the training data within error. At the held-out concentration the root-mean-square error is {{rmse_heldout}}, and the discrepancy is specific rather than diffuse: the model matches the fraction surviving without dividing but over-predicts death and under-predicts division (observed died, divided and survived {{obs_10}}; predicted {{pred_10}}; Fig. 7a), so it captures the ordering of the three concentrations while misplacing the split between death and division at the intermediate one.

Six parameters fitted to six numbers are not all determined by them. Moving one parameter at a time from the fitted values, with the others held there, the fate fractions constrain the death parameters to within about {{prof_hmax}} but leave the two switching rates free over roughly {{prof_switch}} (Supplementary Fig. 3a), which is why the memory of the resistant state was given a prior rather than left to the fate data. {{memslice_sentence}} The parameters quoted above should therefore be read as one member of a family of fits that describe these fate fractions equally well, and the biological reading of the memory rests on the lineage correlations that set the prior, not on the fate fractions. Halving the integration step changes the simulated fractions by {{dt_shift}}, less than the seed-to-seed spread, so the discretisation is not a factor (Supplementary Fig. 3c).

A second mechanism can produce the same fates. Cisplatin kills preferentially during replication, so where a cell sits in its division cycle when the drug arrives is a competing explanation both for the dose-invariance of death timing and for the cells that survive three days without dividing: a hazard confined to part of the cycle spares whatever never enters it, with no heritable state at all. We therefore refitted the model with a cell-cycle-dependent hazard, adding one parameter, the fraction of the hazard that is independent of cycle position, with killing otherwise peaking at mid-cycle, under the same objective, bounds, budget and seeds (Methods). {{cycle_result}} (Supplementary Note 12 and Supplementary Fig. 4). Three fate counts per concentration cannot separate inherited protection from cell-cycle position; the measurement that can is the cycle position of each cell at the moment the drug arrives, which a cycle reporter records directly.

Properties that were not fitted can then be compared with the data. The predicted kill curves (Fig. 7b) are {{kc_ratio}}-fold steeper at 13 µM in their first two days than in their second half, but they do not plateau, as HCT116 cells do after about 100 h. Death fates are correlated between sisters ({{phi_sis}}), first cousins ({{phi_c1}}) and second cousins ({{phi_c2}}), and the correlation decays to {{phi_c3}} for third cousins ({{phi_c3_ci}} over three seeds, a range that contains zero) and {{phi_unrel}} for unrelated cells (Fig. 7c). That is the pattern reported for HCT116 lineages, in which fate correlations are present for first and second cousins and absent for third cousins, with memory inherited over at least two to three generations [@iyer2025]; the comparison is qualitative on both sides, because the source reports these correlations graphically and gives no numeric coefficients or confidence intervals to match. One caveat belongs beside this agreement. Cells here draw their cycle times independently, so no cycle timing is inherited and every correlation between related cells is carried by the inherited promoter state. Cell-cycle duration is in fact heritable over several generations, and its inheritance alone produces strong cousin and second-cousin correlations even when mother-daughter correlations are weak [@sandler2015; @kuchen2020; @chakrabarti2018]. Coupled to a cycle-gated hazard, that is a complete alternative explanation for the correlations fitted here, and the model as built cannot produce it. The mean time to death changes by {{deathtime_shift}} between 7 and 13 µM while the death fraction rises from {{obs_died_7}} to {{obs_died_13}} (Fig. 7d), the dose-invariance of single-cell timing that motivated the original study, and it arises here because dose changes the fraction of unprotected cells rather than their kinetics. The plateau the kill curves miss is the clearest failure of the minimal model: reproducing it requires cells to stay in the resistant state for longer than the memory of {{cal_memgen}} generations allows, and that memory was set by the prior taken from the lineage correlations rather than by the kill curves. The two observations therefore pull in opposite directions within this minimal model: correlations that vanish by the third cousin call for a short memory, and a kill curve that flattens calls for a long one. A single switching gene cannot satisfy both, which is a concrete and testable limitation, and points to a drug-induced stabilisation of the resistant state, for which there is independent evidence [@shaffer2017], as the mechanism a lineage-tracing experiment should look for. The calibration landscape (Fig. 7e) shows how the fitted death rate and EC$_{50}$ are constrained by the fate fractions alone; the protocol applies to any observable a laboratory records.

## Schedule optimisation is consistent with clinical outcomes

With a calibrated model the framework becomes a schedule testbed. We first asked which schedule best delays regrowth of a melanoma-like population with a rare, heritable pre-resistant state [@shaffer2017] treated at a fractional-killing dose, comparing continuous dosing, the intermittent schedule of the SWOG S1320 trial, in which patients received a continuous 8-week lead-in and were then randomised to continuous dosing or to cycles of 3 weeks off and 5 weeks on [@algazi2020], and adaptive therapy, in which the drug is withdrawn when the population has halved and resumed when it regrows to its initial size [@zhang2017adaptive]. Because adaptive therapy deliberately lets the tumour regrow, schedules are compared by the time after randomisation at which the population exceeds 120% of its pre-treatment size (loss of control), with the RECIST-like time to progression from the nadir reported alongside (Methods).

The answer depends on two properties of the resistant cells (Fig. 8a–d). When the resistant state is fully protective and carries no fitness cost, as lineage data indicate for pre-existing tolerance [@iyer2025], resistant cells grow at the same rate with or without drug, so a holiday cannot cost the resistant subpopulation anything and can only let sensitive cells regrow to be killed again. Even so, both interrupted schedules keep control longer than continuous dosing: the three lose control after {{ttp_cont_nocost}}, {{ttp_int_nocost}} and {{ttp_adapt_nocost}} weeks, with the intermittent schedule delivering {{dose_int_nocost}} of the cumulative dose of continuous dosing over the full 60-week simulation. The reason is a second drug effect rather than the fitness of resistant cells: the drug stabilises the resistant state tenfold by suppressing the promoter inactivation rate (Methods), so continuous exposure keeps cells resistant that a holiday allows to revert. That effect is set in the model, not fitted, and it is what makes holidays worth anything at all in this arm. When resistant cells pay a 50% fitness cost in the absence of drug, a coarse stand-in for the drug dependence that made intermittent dosing superior in vemurafenib-treated patient-derived xenografts, where withdrawal drove established resistant tumours to regress rather than merely grow more slowly [@dasthakur2013], holidays let sensitive cells outcompete them: none of the three schedules loses control within the 52-week follow-up ({{ttp_cont_cost}}, {{ttp_int_cost}} and {{ttp_adapt_cost}} weeks, censored), and adaptive therapy achieves this with {{dose_adapt_cost}} of the cumulative dose of continuous dosing. When resistant cells are only partly protected and grow at half speed under drug, holidays release them: continuous dosing keeps control for {{ttp_cont_partial}} weeks against {{ttp_int_partial}} for the intermittent schedule and {{ttp_adapt_partial}} for adaptive therapy, the ranking observed in the trial, where continuous dosing gave a median progression-free survival of 9.0 months against 5.5 months for intermittent dosing. Gating three quarters of the death hazard to a window of the cell cycle, as in the persister simulations above, leaves these comparisons standing, whether or not the maximal hazard is raised to hold the cycle-averaged hazard fixed (Supplementary Note 13 and Supplementary Fig. 5): {{cycle_mel_result}}. The memory of the resistant state, five net population doublings, is an assumption rather than a measurement, and it is what decides whether resistant cells revert during a three-week holiday; scanning it from two to twelve doublings leaves the ranking unchanged and narrows the advantage of holidays from {{memory_scan_range}} (Supplementary Note 15). That the benefit of holidays should depend on a cost of resistance is established by compartment models of adaptive therapy, which also show that tumour turnover modulates how large that cost must be [@strobl2021]. Here the dependence emerges from single cells whose resistance is a heritable expression state rather than a fixed compartment, the cost and the degree of protection are properties of cells that lineage experiments measure, and the third case shows that partial protection reverses the ranking even when a cost is absent. The optimiser makes the dependence explicit (Fig. 8e): over intermittent schedules of any period and duty cycle after the lead-in, the best schedule is {{opt_nocost}} without a fitness cost, {{opt_cost}} with a fitness cost and {{opt_partial}} with partial protection. A bacterial-persistence model adapted to colorectal-cancer lines reaches the same conclusion by another route: an intermediate dose with release periods is optimal, because the rate of transition into the persistent state rises with concentration and then saturates [@corigliano2025]. The agreement is partial and the difference is informative. Where tolerance is drug-induced, the optimiser here also selects an intermediate dose over the highest one, for the same reason. Release periods, however, help there only when the drug changes the kinetics of the resistant state, either by stabilising it, as in the melanoma arm above, or by inducing it at a rate that saturates, as in that model; with a state whose decay rate is the same with and without drug, a holiday merely postpones the death of reverting cells while every cell grows, and the optimiser rejects it. The model therefore reproduces both the clinical and the preclinical outcome and shows their difference to depend on measurable properties of the resistant cells, their growth under drug and their fitness without it, both properties of cells rather than of the tumour, which growth and lineage experiments can bound, within the identifiability limits of Fig. 6c, before a schedule is chosen. The mechanism is set in each case, not inferred from the outcome, so this is a demonstration that the ranking follows from cell properties rather than a claim that these particular cells carry them; and because the trial and the xenograft also differ in drug and species (dabrafenib plus trametinib in patients, vemurafenib monotherapy in mice), their contrast is not attributable to resistant-cell fitness alone.

The second case study concerns glioblastoma, where the RTOG 0525 trial compared the standard temozolomide regimen (150–200 mg/m² on days 1–5 of 28-day cycles) with a dose-dense regimen (75–100 mg/m² on days 1–21) that delivers about twice the cumulative dose and was designed to deplete MGMT, and found no difference in survival for either MGMT-methylated or unmethylated tumours [@gilbert2013]. We simulated both regimens with one-compartment pharmacokinetics (elimination half-life 2.1 h, a plasma value from a three-compartment population model of plasma and cerebrospinal fluid [@ostermann2004]; an independent single-dose study gives 1.8 h [@rudek2004]) on populations in which 1% (methylated) or 30% (unmethylated) of cells express MGMT. Those two fractions are illustrative values that bracket low and high expression, not measurements, and the idealisation matters: promoter methylation shifts MGMT expression as a graded quantity across cells rather than switching a discrete subpopulation on, so the whole dose-dense prediction below rests on treating it as two states. With that caveat, with MGMT protein protecting cells from the drug [@lasri2020] and either stable or consumed by it stoichiometrically, one molecule per lesion repaired, as a suicide enzyme is, so that the pool follows the cumulative exposure rather than the peak concentration (Fig. 8f,g). Lesions are not tracked as a state variable: they are taken to form in proportion to dose and to be repaired at once, so "one molecule per lesion" enters only through the rate of consumption, and no unrepaired damage accumulates. The rate constant of that consumption is not fitted to data; it is set so that consumption at the standard bolus peak matches the first-order parameterisation it replaces, which makes the two mechanisms comparable at the same initial rate and isolates how each integrates over time. With stable MGMT, the depth of response over six cycles is {{lk_meth_stable_std}} log$_{10}$ units for the standard regimen and {{lk_meth_stable_dense}} for the dose-dense regimen in the methylated population ({{lk_unmeth_stable_std}} and {{lk_unmeth_stable_dense}} in the unmethylated one), and a 21-day regimen at the standard cumulative dose achieves no measurable kill ({{lk_meth_stable_equal}} log$_{10}$ units): the death hazard rises steeply with concentration, so spreading the same dose over more days loses efficacy, and the advantage of the dose-dense arm in the model is entirely its larger cumulative dose. With stable MGMT the two arms therefore differ little in the unmethylated population ({{lk_unmeth_stable_std}} against {{lk_unmeth_stable_dense}} log$_{10}$ units), as in the trial. Consumption changes that. Because one molecule is spent per lesion repaired, the pool follows the cumulative exposure, which the dose-dense regimen more than doubles: in the methylated population its response deepens to {{lk_meth_consumed_dense}} log$_{10}$ units against {{lk_meth_consumed_std}} for the standard regimen, and in the unmethylated population, where 30% of cells start protected, the same depletion turns a regimen that barely worked into one that controls the population ({{lk_unmeth_consumed_dense}} against {{lk_unmeth_consumed_std}} log$_{10}$ units; final size {{nend_unmeth_consumed_dense}} against {{nend_unmeth_consumed_std}} times the initial size, and {{nend_unmeth_stable_std}} and {{nend_unmeth_stable_dense}} with stable MGMT). Modelled as the suicide enzyme it is, MGMT therefore predicts a clear dose-dense advantage in both populations, which RTOG 0525 did not find. Read as a bound rather than as a fit, that disagreement is informative: either temozolomide consumes MGMT in tumours far more slowly than this stoichiometry allows, or the benefit of depleting it is offset by something the model leaves out. At the standard cumulative dose the number of dosing days per cycle that minimises the final population is {{days_methylated_stable}} (methylated) and {{days_unmethylated_stable}} (unmethylated) with stable MGMT, and {{days_methylated_consumed}} and {{days_unmethylated_consumed}} when MGMT is consumed (Fig. 8h). These simulations are not fits to the trial, whose end points depend on much that the model omits, dose-limiting toxicity and the dose reductions it forces among it. What they add is a sharper version of the rationale for dose-dense dosing: the benefit it is supposed to deliver exists in the model only at consumption rates that the trial's null result appears to exclude. The quantity the argument rests on is the per-lesion molecular rate constant of that consumption, which has not been measured directly, although bulk depletion time-courses have: protracted temozolomide lowers alkyltransferase activity in peripheral blood mononuclear cells by about 63% at 14 days and 73% at 21 days [@tolcher2003], a compressed 1,000 mg/m² schedule takes it to 18% of initial levels while O⁶-methylguanine accumulates in both blood cells and tumour biopsies [@middleton2000], and in orthotopic GBM43 xenografts tumour MGMT activity is depleted by day 6 on both the standard and the 21-day schedule and stays suppressed only on the 21-day one [@robinson2010]. The last of these is the sharpest comparison available, and it makes the disagreement more specific than the trial alone does: the deeper and longer tumour depletion that the dose-dense schedule is supposed to deliver was confirmed in that model, and survival still did not improve. Either the depletion measured in bulk is not the per-cell depletion that matters for killing, or the benefit of depleting MGMT is offset by something the model leaves out.
