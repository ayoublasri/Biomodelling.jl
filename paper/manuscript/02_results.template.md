# Results

## A layered simulator of growing, dividing, treated cells

Biomodelling.jl 2.0 is organised in layers (Fig. 1). A model is a list of reactions with mass-action, Hill or custom kinetics and named parameters; promoter states are explicit species, grouped so that they are inherited at division and doubled at replication. Four kernels advance a cell's state at fixed volume: the exact direct method, fixed-step tau-leaping, a hybrid scheme that falls back to the exact method whenever a leap would produce a negative count, and adaptive tau-leaping with critical-reaction handling. The population layer grows each cell's volume exponentially, scales propensities with volume so that concentrations are invariant, replicates genes at a set point of the cycle, divides cells by a sizer, adder or timer rule with binomial or over-dispersed partitioning, and controls the population size by Moran-like replacement, free growth or a logistic hazard. A perturbation layer applies dose schedules, state-dependent death, growth inhibition, drug-induced rate changes and genetic perturbations. Every cell's ancestry, fate and state at birth and division enter a lineage table, from which heritability, autocorrelation, memory timescales, fluctuation-test and clonal-memory statistics follow. Observation models convert true counts into scRNA-seq, smFISH or time-lapse measurements, and the same forward model drives likelihood-free inference. Table 1 sets these capabilities against those of the simulators most used to benchmark single-cell methods.

## The engine is exact and reproducible in another package

We first verified the kernels against exact stationary laws (Fig. 2). For a birth-death process the simulated distribution of 50,000 cells is indistinguishable from the Poisson law (Fig. 2a); for the telegraph model in an intermediate and in a bursty regime it matches the Beta-Poisson distribution [@peccoud1995] (Fig. 2b); and for bursty protein production it matches the negative binomial (Fig. 2c). Across kernels, Kolmogorov-Smirnov distances to the exact law with 20,000 cells lie below the 99% critical value for the direct method, for the adaptive scheme at both tolerances and for the hybrid scheme at the shorter step, and above it for the hybrid scheme at the longer step ({{ks_hybrid_coarse}} against a critical value of {{ks_crit20k}}; Fig. 2d). That is the expected behaviour of an approximate kernel, and it sets the step below which the approximation is not detectable at this sample size; wall-clock costs are {{fig2d_times}} per 10⁴ cells. Simulating the same telegraph model with JumpProcesses.jl gives distributions whose two-sample Kolmogorov-Smirnov statistic against Biomodelling.jl is {{ks_two}} (99% critical value {{ks_crit}}; Fig. 2e), with a {{speed_ratio}} difference in serial wall-clock time. Population simulations scale linearly with the number of cells and approximately linearly with the number of reactions: {{runtime_10k}} for {{runtime_case}} with four threads (Fig. 2f).

## Physiology alone generates memory, size scaling and cell-cycle effects

The physiology of the population layer suffices to produce properties of single-cell data that phenomenological simulators must impose by hand (Fig. 3). In a single lineage of a slowly switching gene, mRNA and protein track volume within a cycle and are halved at division, while the promoter state, inherited by both daughters, persists over many divisions (Fig. 3a). For a fast-switching gene without replication, transcript numbers scale linearly with cell volume, so that concentration is homeostatic; adding mid-cycle gene replication doubles transcription in the second half of the cycle and lifts counts of large cells above the volume-scaling line (Fig. 3b), the copy-number effect that mechanistic fits to scRNA-seq data must account for [@sukys2025]. Correlations of protein concentration at division between mothers and daughters, sisters and cousins fall from {{md_slow}} to {{md_fast}} as the promoter switching rate crosses the inverse cell-cycle time (Fig. 3c), with sisters more correlated than mother-daughter pairs and cousins less, and mRNA less heritable than protein. The memory timescale extracted from the generation-resolved lineage autocorrelation is set by the promoter switching time and is nearly independent of the cell-cycle time as long as memory outlasts the cycle; when the two are comparable the estimate is bounded below by one cell cycle, the resolution of the autocorrelation (Fig. 3d). As reported for real lineages [@zhang2025], the coefficient of variation of a stable protein's concentration is larger across the population than along single lineages, whether or not cell-cycle times are noisy, and over-dispersed partitioning adds variability in both perspectives (Fig. 3e). Telegraph parameters fitted separately to cells before and after replication differ by the expected factor of two in burst frequency, and a fit that ignores the cell cycle returns intermediate values (Fig. 3f).

## One resistance gene reproduces persister phenomenology

To test whether heritable expression states suffice to reproduce the phenomenology of drug-tolerant persisters, we coupled a resistance gene with slow promoter switching (memory timescale {{mem_time}} time units, cell-cycle time 20) to a death hazard that rises with dose and is suppressed by the concentration of the gene's protein product (Methods). Under continuous, pulsed and reduced-dose schedules the population first collapses as low-expressing cells die, then regrows from high-expressing survivors (Fig. 4a); kill curves are biphasic with a surviving fraction that falls with dose (Fig. 4b). Between doses 0.5 and 2 the net rate of population decline over the first 30 time units of treatment rises from {{decay_lo}} to {{decay_hi}} per time unit ({{decay_fold}}-fold), while the mean division time of cells born under drug changes by {{divtime_change}} and the mean time to death of killed cells moves only from {{deathtime_range}} time units (Fig. 4c). This reproduces the observation that population decay depends strongly on dose even though single-cell division and death times barely do [@iyer2025], because fate is decided by states that exist before the drug arrives.

Related cells accordingly share the fate of their lineages. Among cells born in the 25 time units before the drug, one whose sister founded a surviving lineage does so itself with probability {{sis_cond}}, against a marginal probability of {{sis_marg}} ({{sis_fold}}-fold enrichment over {{sis_n}} sister pairs; {{cous_fold}}-fold for cousins), whereas in a fast-switching control, in which survival is rarer, the conditional and marginal probabilities coincide ({{sis_cond_fast}} versus {{sis_marg_fast}}; Fig. 4d). When tolerance pre-exists, survivors descend from a minority of founder clones and the effective number of clones drops from {{clones_before}} to {{clones_after_pre}}; when tolerance is drug-induced, so that the drug raises the promoter activation rate of every cell, barcode diversity is largely preserved ({{clones_after_ind}}; Fig. 4e). This is the signature that distinguishes the two origins in barcoding experiments [@shaffer2017; @iyer2025].

Disrupting expression memory changes the outcome, and when it is disrupted matters. Accelerating promoter switching 20-fold from two cell-cycle times before the drug, as memory-disrupting epigenetic compounds do [@harmange2023], does not reduce the number of surviving clones if the acceleration stops when the drug arrives: it raises them, from {{clones_none}} to {{clones_predrug}} ({{clones_predrug_p}}). Reshuffling spreads the resistant state over more lineages and moves the resistant fraction towards its stationary value ({{resfrac_none}} to {{resfrac_predrug}}), so more clones carry a protected cell when the drug arrives, even though protection itself is untouched. Maintaining it during exposure reduces the count to {{clones_during}} clones ({{cells_during}} cells, against {{cells_none}}; Fig. 4f), because resistant cells now leave the protected state within a fraction of the time they need to found a colony. In the model, memory must be disrupted while the drug is present. Compartment theory for phenotypic switching already predicts that an agent which perturbs the retention of the resistant state can turn treatment failure into success [@gunnarsson2020]; what the simulations add is the timing requirement and a lineage-resolved readout, the number of surviving clones, by which it can be tested. A transient pulse enriches high-expressing cells and, with slow switching, the enrichment persists for many generations after the drug is removed, whereas with fast switching it relaxes within a few cycles (Fig. 4g): phenotypic selection with stable inheritance, as in the original MGMT model [@lasri2020]. Finally, scanning release periods and doses of intermittent schedules (Fig. 4h), the most effective schedule is {{best_pre}} for pre-existing tolerance, {{best_cost}} when the resistant state carries a fitness cost, and {{best_ind}} for drug-induced tolerance. Without a fitness cost nothing beats continuous dosing at the highest dose, since every release period lets the resistant subpopulation regrow. With a fitness cost the population declines under every schedule at dose ≥ 0.5, and release periods of up to 10 time units match continuous dosing ({{cont_cost}} per time unit under continuous dose 2), because cells that leave the resistant state during a holiday are killed on re-exposure. For drug-induced tolerance the ranking of doses reverses: the highest dose induces tolerance in more cells than it kills, so dose 1 outperforms dose 2 at every release period ({{best_ind}}, against {{cont_ind}} for continuous dose 2), the intermediate-dose optimum reported previously [@corigliano2025]; the benefit of release periods reported in that work does not appear here, because in this model the induced state decays at the same rate with and without drug, so a holiday only postpones the death of reverting cells while all cells grow.

## Ground truth for memory genes, networks and perturbations

Because every cell's clone, lineage and true state are known, the framework supplies ground truth that observational data cannot (Fig. 5). For 40 independent genes whose memory times span three decades, MemorySeq-style clonal variance scores computed on true concentrations rise from {{score_min}} for genes with memory far below the cell-cycle time to {{score_max}} for the slowest genes, and the ranking survives computation on sequenced counts at 20% capture efficiency (Fig. 5a); the score separates genes with memory longer than the cycle from the rest with an area under the receiver operating curve of {{auc_true}} on true concentrations and {{auc_seq}} on sequenced counts. For a 30-gene network simulated at fixed volume, in a dividing population and after sequencing, correlation-based and GENIE3 inference lose most of their accuracy in the dividing population when raw counts are used (area under the precision-recall curve {{aupr_pearson_fixed}} to {{aupr_pearson_counts}} for Pearson correlation and {{aupr_genie3_fixed}} to {{aupr_genie3_counts}} for GENIE3, against {{aupr_random_undir}} for a random ranking of undirected pairs and {{aupr_random_dir}} for directed edges), because volume and gene copy number induce positive correlations between all genes. Normalising counts to concentrations removes the volume effect but recovers little ({{aupr_pearson_conc}} and {{aupr_genie3_conc}}), since the dependence on copy number and cell age remains; regressing expression on the true cell-cycle covariates, which the simulation provides and which cell-cycle scoring approximates in real data, recovers most of the loss ({{aupr_pearson_reg}} and {{aupr_genie3_reg}}). Sequencing at 15% capture efficiency lowers accuracy further ({{aupr_pearson_seq}} raw and {{aupr_pearson_norm}} library-size-normalised for Pearson correlation; {{aupr_genie3_seq}} and {{aupr_genie3_norm}} for GENIE3; Fig. 5b). Imputing the sequenced counts before inference recovers part of that loss: MAGIC raises the area under the precision-recall curve from {{aupr_pearson_seq}} to {{aupr_p_magic}} for Pearson correlation and from {{aupr_genie3_seq}} to {{aupr_g_magic}} for GENIE3, and kNN-smoothing gives {{aupr_p_knn}} and {{aupr_g_knn}} (Fig. 5c). Neither reaches the accuracy obtained at fixed volume, nor that of regressing out the cell-cycle covariates, so imputation mitigates the sequencing loss without touching the growth confound that produces most of it [@lasri2022]. Knocking down the five most connected regulators of a 20-gene network gives true fold changes that a no-change baseline predicts with a mean coefficient of determination of {{r2_zero}}. Propagating observational correlations beats that baseline for {{r2_corr_nwin}} knockdowns, with coefficients of determination from {{r2_corr_good}}, and fails on the remaining one ({{r2_corr_worst}}), so its mean over the five is {{r2_corr}} against a median of {{r2_corr_med}} (Fig. 5d). A predictor that is useful for most perturbations and badly wrong for one is precisely what an out-of-distribution evaluation has to expose, and exposing it needs the causal ground truth that the simulation supplies [@ahlmanneltze2025; @vollenweider2026].

## Inference recovers parameters and exposes the cost of ignoring division

On a snapshot of 2,000 non-dividing cells, ABC-SMC with log-uniform priors over three decades and the Wasserstein distance between simulated and observed counts [@bernton2019] concentrates on the true telegraph parameters and agrees with the exact maximum-likelihood estimate (posterior medians {{abc_a}} for truth 0.3, 0.6, 20; Fig. 6a), the broadest posterior falling on the promoter inactivation rate, which a single snapshot constrains least. On a snapshot of a growing and dividing population with volume-scaled transcription and mid-cycle replication, the naive telegraph fit is biased ({{naive_b}}) because volume and copy number inflate counts and their dispersion, whereas ABC with the population simulator as forward model recovers the truth ({{abc_b}}; Fig. 6b). Drug parameters follow from population data in the same way: from a kill curve and the concordance of sister fates, the posterior over the maximal death rate and the protection threshold is a ridge (weighted correlation of the two log parameters {{ridge_corr}}) that contains the truth (Fig. 6c), and posterior predictive kill curves simulated at the posterior median reproduce the observed one (Fig. 6d). The two summaries constrain the product of death rate and unprotected fraction, so separating the parameters requires measuring the protective protein: an experimental-design question the forward model answers before an experiment is run.

## Calibration to time-lapse data predicts held-out conditions

A simulator is useful for treatment questions only if it can be fitted to the data a laboratory produces and then predict what it was not fitted to. We therefore calibrated the resistance-gene model to time-lapse measurements in which U2OS cells were tracked for three days under 7, 10 and 13 µM cisplatin and every cell present at drug addition was scored as dying, dividing or surviving without dividing [@iyer2025] (Supplementary Table 4). Six parameters, the two promoter switching rates, the maximal death rate, the EC$_{50}$ and Hill coefficient of death and the IC$_{50}$ of growth arrest, were fitted to the fate fractions at 7 and 13 µM by simulated moments with common random numbers, under a weak prior keeping the memory of the resistant state near the two to three generations that the same study inferred from lineage correlations (Methods). The calibrated model (memory {{cal_memgen}} generations, resistant fraction {{cal_pon}}, EC$_{50}$ {{cal_ec50}} µM; Fig. 7f) reproduces the training fractions with a root-mean-square error of {{rmse_train}}. That error should be read against the precision of the measurement: the observed fractions carry a mean binomial standard error of {{binom_se}} and repeated simulations differ by {{sim_se}}, so the fit is about {{rmse_train_in_se}} times the combined noise and the model does not reproduce the training data within error. At the held-out concentration the root-mean-square error is {{rmse_heldout}}, and the discrepancy is specific rather than diffuse: the model matches the fraction surviving without dividing but over-predicts death and under-predicts division (observed died, divided and survived {{obs_10}}; predicted {{pred_10}}; Fig. 7a), so it captures the ordering of the three concentrations while misplacing the split between death and division at the intermediate one.

Six parameters fitted to six numbers are not all determined by them. Moving one parameter at a time from the fitted values, with the others held there, the fate fractions constrain the death parameters to within about {{prof_hmax}} but leave the two switching rates free over roughly {{prof_switch}} (Supplementary Fig. 3a), which is why the memory of the resistant state was given a prior rather than left to the fate data. {{memslice_sentence}} The parameters quoted above should therefore be read as one member of a family of fits that describe these fate fractions equally well, and the biological reading of the memory rests on the lineage correlations that set the prior, not on the fate fractions. Halving the integration step changes the simulated fractions by {{dt_shift}}, less than the seed-to-seed spread, so the discretisation is not a factor (Supplementary Fig. 3c).

Properties that were not fitted can then be compared with the data. Death fates are correlated between sisters ({{phi_sis}}), first cousins ({{phi_c1}}) and second cousins ({{phi_c2}}), and the correlation decays to {{phi_c3}} for third cousins and {{phi_unrel}} for unrelated cells (Fig. 7c), the pattern reported for HCT116 lineages. The mean time to death changes by {{deathtime_shift}} between 7 and 13 µM while the death fraction rises from {{obs_died_7}} to {{obs_died_13}} (Fig. 7d), the dose-invariance of single-cell timing that motivated the original study, and it arises here because dose changes the fraction of unprotected cells rather than their kinetics. The predicted kill curves (Fig. 7b) are {{kc_ratio}}-fold steeper at 13 µM in their first two days than in their second half, but they do not plateau, as HCT116 cells do after about 100 h. In the model the plateau requires cells to stay in the resistant state for longer than the memory of {{cal_memgen}} generations allows, and that memory was set by the prior taken from the lineage correlations rather than by the kill curves. The two observations therefore pull in opposite directions within this minimal model: correlations that vanish by the third cousin call for a short memory, and a kill curve that flattens calls for a long one. A single switching gene cannot satisfy both, which is a concrete and testable limitation, and points to a drug-induced stabilisation of the resistant state, for which there is independent evidence [@shaffer2017], as the mechanism a lineage-tracing experiment should look for. The calibration landscape (Fig. 7e) shows how the fitted death rate and EC$_{50}$ are constrained by the fate fractions alone; the protocol applies to any observable a laboratory records.

## Schedule optimisation reproduces and explains clinical outcomes

With a calibrated model the framework becomes a schedule testbed. We first asked which schedule best delays regrowth of a melanoma-like population with a rare, heritable pre-resistant state [@shaffer2017] treated at a fractional-killing dose, comparing continuous dosing, the intermittent schedule of the SWOG S1320 trial, in which patients received a continuous 8-week lead-in and were then randomised to continuous dosing or to cycles of 3 weeks off and 5 weeks on [@algazi2020], and adaptive therapy, in which the drug is withdrawn when the population has halved and resumed when it regrows to its initial size [@zhang2017adaptive]. Because adaptive therapy deliberately lets the tumour regrow, schedules are compared by the time after randomisation at which the population exceeds 120% of its pre-treatment size (loss of control), with the RECIST-like time to progression from the nadir reported alongside (Methods).

The answer depends on two properties of the resistant cells (Fig. 8a–d). When the resistant state is fully protective and carries no fitness cost, as lineage data indicate for pre-existing tolerance [@iyer2025], resistant cells grow at the same rate with or without drug, holidays only let sensitive cells regrow and be killed again, and the three schedules lose control after {{ttp_cont_nocost}}, {{ttp_int_nocost}} and {{ttp_adapt_nocost}} weeks, the intermittent schedule delivering {{dose_int_nocost}} of the cumulative dose. When resistant cells pay a 50% fitness cost in the absence of drug, the drug addiction that made intermittent dosing superior in patient-derived xenografts [@dasthakur2013], holidays let sensitive cells outcompete them: none of the three schedules loses control within the 52-week follow-up ({{ttp_cont_cost}}, {{ttp_int_cost}} and {{ttp_adapt_cost}} weeks, censored), and adaptive therapy achieves this with {{dose_adapt_cost}} of the cumulative dose of continuous dosing. When resistant cells are only partly protected and grow at half speed under drug, holidays release them: continuous dosing keeps control for {{ttp_cont_partial}} weeks against {{ttp_int_partial}} for the intermittent schedule and {{ttp_adapt_partial}} for adaptive therapy, the ranking observed in the trial, where continuous dosing gave a median progression-free survival of 9.0 months against 5.5 months for intermittent dosing. That the benefit of holidays should depend on a cost of resistance is established by compartment models of adaptive therapy, which also show that tumour turnover modulates how large that cost must be [@strobl2021]. Here the dependence emerges from single cells whose resistance is a heritable expression state rather than a fixed compartment, the cost and the degree of protection are properties of cells that lineage experiments measure, and the third case shows that partial protection reverses the ranking even when a cost is absent. The optimiser makes the dependence explicit (Fig. 8e): over intermittent schedules of any period and duty cycle after the lead-in, the best schedule is {{opt_nocost}} without a fitness cost, {{opt_cost}} with a fitness cost and {{opt_partial}} with partial protection. The model therefore reproduces both the clinical and the preclinical outcome and attributes their difference to measurable properties of the resistant cells, their growth under drug and their fitness without it, both of which are properties of cells rather than of the tumour, and which growth and lineage experiments can bound, within the identifiability limits of Fig. 6c, before a schedule is chosen.

The second case study concerns glioblastoma, where the RTOG 0525 trial compared the standard temozolomide regimen (150–200 mg m^-2^ on days 1–5 of 28-day cycles) with a dose-dense regimen (75–100 mg m^-2^ on days 1–21) that delivers about twice the cumulative dose and was designed to deplete MGMT, and found no difference in survival for either MGMT-methylated or unmethylated tumours [@gilbert2013]. We simulated both regimens with one-compartment pharmacokinetics (elimination half-life 2.1 h; ref. [@ostermann2004]) on populations in which 1% (methylated) or 30% (unmethylated) of cells express MGMT, with MGMT protein protecting cells from the drug [@lasri2020] and either stable or consumed by it, as a suicide enzyme is (Fig. 8f,g). With stable MGMT, the depth of response over six cycles is {{lk_meth_stable_std}} log$_{10}$ units for the standard regimen and {{lk_meth_stable_dense}} for the dose-dense regimen in the methylated population ({{lk_unmeth_stable_std}} and {{lk_unmeth_stable_dense}} in the unmethylated one), and a 21-day regimen at the standard cumulative dose achieves no measurable kill ({{lk_meth_stable_equal}} log$_{10}$ units): the death hazard rises steeply with concentration, so spreading the same dose over more days loses efficacy, and the advantage of the dose-dense arm in the model is entirely its larger cumulative dose. When the drug consumes MGMT, prolonged exposure depletes the protection of expressing cells: in the methylated population the dose-dense regimen deepens its response to {{lk_meth_consumed_dense}} log$_{10}$ units against {{lk_meth_consumed_std}} for the standard regimen, and in the unmethylated population, where 30% of cells start protected, both regimens improve (final size {{nend_unmeth_consumed_std}} and {{nend_unmeth_consumed_dense}} times the initial size, against {{nend_unmeth_stable_std}} and {{nend_unmeth_stable_dense}} with stable MGMT) but neither controls the population, so the difference between the arms stays small ({{lk_unmeth_consumed_std}} against {{lk_unmeth_consumed_dense}} log$_{10}$ units), as in the trial. At the standard cumulative dose the number of dosing days per cycle that minimises the final population is {{days_methylated_stable}} (methylated) and {{days_unmethylated_stable}} (unmethylated) with stable MGMT, and {{days_methylated_consumed}} and {{days_unmethylated_consumed}} when MGMT is consumed (Fig. 8h). These simulations are not fits to the trial, whose end points depend on much that the model omits, dose-limiting toxicity among it, but they turn the rationale of dose-dense dosing into a quantitative prediction and identify the measurement that decides between the regimens: how fast the drug consumes MGMT in the tumour.
