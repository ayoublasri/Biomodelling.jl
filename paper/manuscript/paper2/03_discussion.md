# Discussion

The same dosing question has opposite answers in these simulations depending on three properties of the resistant cells, and a trial result and a xenograft result that appeared to contradict each other are both reproduced once those properties are set, the xenograft result as a lower burden at the end of the simulation rather than as a longer time to loss of control. The properties are set in each case rather than inferred from the outcomes, so what the case studies show is that the ranking follows from cell properties, not that any particular tumour has them. Neither anchor is as one-sided as a single end point makes it look. In SWOG S1320 the progression-free survival advantage of continuous dosing (median 9.0 against 5.5 months, hazard ratio 1.36 for intermittent against continuous, $P = 0.063$ against the trial's pre-specified two-sided $\alpha$ of 0.2) did not carry through to overall survival, a secondary end point for which the trial was not powered and on which the two arms were equal at a median of 29.2 months [@algazi2020]; and in the xenografts the resistant tumours were drug-addicted, regressing on withdrawal rather than merely growing more slowly [@dasthakur2013], which is the extreme of the fitness cost that the second mechanism here represents. The two studies also differ in drug and species, so their contrast is not attributable to resistant-cell fitness alone.

What a single-cell forward model adds to the compartment models that established the principle is that the deciding quantities stop being parameters of the model and become properties of cells. Growth under drug, fitness without it and drug-induced stabilisation of the resistant state are all measurable in the growth and lineage-tracing experiments that are now routine, so a schedule question can be narrowed before a trial is designed rather than settled after one. That is the practical claim of this Article, and it is bounded in an important way: the companion Article shows that fitting this model to fate fractions alone leaves its memory timescale undetermined and admits a cycle-gated hazard as an equally good explanation. The properties that decide a schedule are measurable, but they have not been measured here, and the optimiser is only worth running on parameters an experiment has pinned down.

The glioblastoma study is the sharper case, because the model and the trial disagree in two different ways and only one of them is informative. Modelled as the suicide enzyme it is, MGMT creates a dose-dense advantage in unmethylated tumours where the stable model, like the trial, ranked the two regimens as equivalent, so the trial's null bounds the per-lesion rate of consumption there. In methylated tumours the model separates the regimens on cumulative dose alone, with MGMT stable, and the trial found no advantage there either; that discrepancy constrains the model's dose response and the exposure it assumes was delivered, not the rate of consumption. Reporting the second as though it were the first would turn a limitation into a result. The clinical case studies use literature values for cell-cycle times, resistant fractions and pharmacokinetics rather than patient-specific measurements, so their conclusions concern mechanisms and rankings, not absolute survival times. In the glioblastoma study MGMT is consumed stoichiometrically, one molecule per lesion repaired, so its depletion follows the cumulative exposure as a suicide enzyme's should. Two things about that model should be read with care. Lesions are not a state variable: they are assumed to form in proportion to dose and to be repaired immediately, so "one molecule per lesion" enters only through the consumption rate and no unrepaired damage accumulates. And the rate constant of that consumption was set so that the initial consumption rate at the standard bolus peak matches the first-order parameterisation it replaces, which makes the two mechanisms comparable but leaves the constant itself unmeasured; bulk depletion time-courses in blood cells and in tumour tissue exist [@tolcher2003; @middleton2000; @robinson2010] and bound it loosely, and that is why we name the per-lesion constant as the quantity that separates the regimens in unmethylated tumours. In methylated tumours the model separates them even with MGMT stable, on cumulative dose alone, and RTOG 0525 found no advantage there either; that discrepancy constrains the model's dose response and the exposure it assumes was delivered, not the rate of consumption. MGMT is in any case only one determinant of a temozolomide response that is also mismatch-repair-dependent, and the 1% and 30% expressing fractions are illustrative values that bracket low and high expression rather than measured quantities: promoter methylation shifts expression as a graded quantity across cells rather than switching a discrete subpopulation on, and the dose-dense prediction rests entirely on this two-state idealisation. The memory of the resistant state is expressed in these tumour-scale studies as a number of net population doublings, not of cell divisions, and so is not the division-counted memory of memory-sequencing experiments.

Because the framework simulates the lineage-resolved data from which the deciding properties are measured, the natural next step is not a larger scan but an experiment: a lineage-tracing measurement in which the growth of resistant cells with and without drug, and the stability of the resistant state under exposure, are read off the same tree. Those three numbers are what stand between a schedule hypothesis and a schedule prediction.
