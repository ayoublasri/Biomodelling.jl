## Results

## Treatment holidays help or hurt according to three cell properties

The forward model used here is the one described in the companion Article: stochastic reaction kinetics inside cells that grow, replicate their genes and divide, with a single resistance gene whose promoter switches slowly, whose protein protects the cell through a concentration-dependent death hazard, and whose state both daughters inherit at division. On top of it sits a dose-schedule layer and a schedule optimiser (Methods). What that combination buys is the subject of this Article: a resistance phenotype with measurable single-cell kinetics rather than a compartment with an assumed rate, so that the properties which decide a dosing question become quantities a lineage experiment can bound. We first asked which schedule best delays regrowth of a melanoma-like population with a rare, heritable pre-resistant state [@shaffer2017] treated at a fractional-killing dose, comparing continuous dosing, the intermittent schedule of the SWOG S1320 trial, in which patients received a continuous 8-week lead-in and were then randomised to continuous dosing or to cycles of 3 weeks off and 5 weeks on [@algazi2020], and adaptive therapy, in which the drug is withdrawn when the population has halved and resumed when it regrows to its initial size [@zhang2017adaptive]. Because adaptive therapy deliberately lets the tumour regrow, schedules are compared by the time after randomisation at which the population exceeds 120% of its pre-treatment size (loss of control), with the RECIST-like time to progression from the nadir reported alongside (Methods).

The answer depends on three properties of the resistant cells (Fig. 1a–d): their growth under drug, their fitness without it, and whether the drug stabilises the resistant state. When the resistant state is fully protective and carries no fitness cost, as lineage data indicate for pre-existing tolerance [@iyer2025], resistant cells grow at the same rate with or without drug, so a holiday cannot cost the resistant subpopulation anything and can only let sensitive cells regrow to be killed again. Even so, both interrupted schedules keep control longer than continuous dosing: the three lose control after {{ttp_cont_nocost}}, {{ttp_int_nocost}} and {{ttp_adapt_nocost}} weeks, with the intermittent schedule delivering {{dose_int_nocost}} of the cumulative dose of continuous dosing over the full 60-week simulation. The reason is a second drug effect rather than the fitness of resistant cells: the drug stabilises the resistant state tenfold by suppressing the promoter inactivation rate (Methods), so continuous exposure keeps cells resistant that a holiday allows to revert. That effect is set in the model, not fitted, and it is what makes holidays worth anything at all in this arm. When resistant cells pay a 50% fitness cost in the absence of drug, a coarse stand-in for the drug dependence that made intermittent dosing superior in vemurafenib-treated patient-derived xenografts, where withdrawal drove established resistant tumours to regress rather than merely grow more slowly [@dasthakur2013], holidays let sensitive cells outcompete them. On the declared end point the three schedules are indistinguishable: none loses control within the 52-week follow-up ({{ttp_cont_cost}}, {{ttp_int_cost}} and {{ttp_adapt_cost}} weeks, censored). The xenograft ranking appears instead in the tumour burden at the end of the 60-week simulation, which the intermittent schedule leaves at {{nend_int_cost}} of the pre-treatment size against {{nend_cont_cost}} under continuous dosing, on {{dose_int_cost}} of its cumulative dose; adaptive therapy, which withdraws the drug earliest, ends higher at {{nend_adapt_cost}} but on {{dose_adapt_cost}} of the cumulative dose of continuous dosing. When resistant cells are only partly protected and grow at half speed under drug, holidays release them: continuous dosing keeps control for {{ttp_cont_partial}} weeks against {{ttp_int_partial}} for the intermittent schedule and {{ttp_adapt_partial}} for adaptive therapy, the ranking observed in the trial, where continuous dosing gave a median progression-free survival of 9.0 months against 5.5 months for intermittent dosing. Scored from the nadir instead, as in RECIST, the same simulations give {{ttpn_cont_nocost}}, {{ttpn_int_nocost}} and {{ttpn_adapt_nocost}} weeks for continuous, intermittent and adaptive dosing without a fitness cost, {{ttpn_cont_cost}}, {{ttpn_int_cost}} and {{ttpn_adapt_cost}} weeks with a fitness cost, and {{ttpn_cont_partial}}, {{ttpn_int_partial}} and {{ttpn_adapt_partial}} weeks under partial protection. Adaptive therapy progresses from its nadir almost at once in every mechanism, because the schedule withholds the drug until the population has regrown to its pre-treatment size, and in the fitness-cost arm all three schedules progress from the nadir although none loses control within the follow-up. That discordance is why the schedules are compared by loss of control: an end point referred to the nadir penalises a schedule for the regrowth it is designed to permit. Gating three quarters of the death hazard to a window of the cell cycle, as in the persister simulations of the companion Article, leaves these comparisons standing, whether or not the maximal hazard is raised to hold the cycle-averaged hazard fixed (Supplementary Note 1 and Supplementary Fig. 1): {{cycle_mel_result}}. The memory of the resistant state, five net population doublings, is an assumption rather than a measurement, and it is what decides whether resistant cells revert during a three-week holiday; scanning it from two to twelve doublings leaves the ranking unchanged in the two arms scanned, without and with a fitness cost of resistance, and narrows the advantage of holidays from {{memory_scan_range}}; the partial-protection arm, the one that reproduces the ranking of the trial, was not scanned (Supplementary Note 2). That the benefit of holidays should depend on a cost of resistance is established by compartment models of adaptive therapy, which also show that tumour turnover modulates how large that cost must be [@strobl2021]. Here the dependence emerges from single cells whose resistance is a heritable expression state rather than a fixed compartment, the cost and the degree of protection are properties of cells that lineage experiments measure, and the third case shows that partial protection reverses the ranking even when a cost is absent. The optimiser makes the dependence explicit (Fig. 1e): over intermittent schedules of any period and duty cycle after the lead-in, the best schedule without a fitness cost is {{opt_nocost}}. With a fitness cost, {{opt_cost}}; with partial protection, {{opt_partial}}. With a fitness cost the star in Fig. 1e is therefore the first point of a tie and not an optimum, and what the scan establishes there is that control is kept for the whole follow-up, not which schedule keeps it best; under partial protection the tie is between four grid points that are all continuous dosing, so there the star does mark the winning schedule. A bacterial-persistence model adapted to colorectal-cancer lines reaches the same conclusion by another route: an intermediate dose with release periods is optimal, because the rate of transition into the persistent state rises with concentration and then saturates [@corigliano2025]. The agreement is partial and the difference is informative. Where tolerance is drug-induced, the optimiser here also selects an intermediate dose over the highest one, for the same reason. Release periods, however, help there only when the drug changes the kinetics of the resistant state, either by stabilising it, as in the melanoma arm above, or by inducing it at a rate that saturates, as in that model; with a state whose decay rate is the same with and without drug, a holiday merely postpones the death of reverting cells while every cell grows, and the optimiser rejects it. The model therefore reproduces both the clinical and the preclinical outcome, the preclinical one as a lower burden at the end of the simulation rather than as a longer time to loss of control, and shows their difference to depend on measurable properties of the resistant cells, their growth under drug, their fitness without it and whether the drug stabilises their state, properties of cells rather than of the tumour, which growth and lineage experiments can bound, within the identifiability limits reported in the companion Article, before a schedule is chosen. The mechanism is set in each case, not inferred from the outcome, so this is a demonstration that the ranking follows from cell properties rather than a claim that these particular cells carry them; and because the trial and the xenograft also differ in drug and species (dabrafenib plus trametinib in patients, vemurafenib monotherapy in mice), their contrast is not attributable to resistant-cell fitness alone.

## Consumption of MGMT, not dose, is what the glioblastoma trial bounds

The second case study concerns glioblastoma, where the RTOG 0525 trial compared the standard temozolomide regimen (150–200 mg/m² on days 1–5 of 28-day cycles) with a dose-dense regimen (75–100 mg/m² on days 1–21) that delivers about twice the cumulative dose and was designed to deplete MGMT, and found no difference in survival for either MGMT-methylated or unmethylated tumours [@gilbert2013]. We simulated both regimens with one-compartment pharmacokinetics (elimination half-life 2.1 h, a plasma value from a three-compartment population model of plasma and cerebrospinal fluid [@ostermann2004]; an independent single-dose study gives 1.8 h [@rudek2004]) on populations in which 1% (methylated) or 30% (unmethylated) of cells express MGMT. Those two fractions are illustrative values that bracket low and high expression, not measurements, and the idealisation matters: promoter methylation shifts MGMT expression as a graded quantity across cells rather than switching a discrete subpopulation on, so the whole dose-dense prediction below rests on treating it as two states. With that caveat, with MGMT protein protecting cells from the drug [@lasri2020] and either stable or consumed by it stoichiometrically, one molecule per lesion repaired, as a suicide enzyme is, so that the pool follows the cumulative exposure rather than the peak concentration (Fig. 2a,b). Lesions are not tracked as a state variable: they are taken to form in proportion to dose and to be repaired at once, so "one molecule per lesion" enters only through the rate of consumption, and no unrepaired damage accumulates. The rate constant of that consumption is not fitted to data; it is set so that consumption at the standard bolus peak matches the first-order parameterisation it replaces, which makes the two mechanisms comparable at the same initial rate and isolates how each integrates over time. With stable MGMT, the depth of response over six cycles is {{lk_meth_stable_std}} log$_{10}$ units for the standard regimen and {{lk_meth_stable_dense}} for the dose-dense regimen in the methylated population ({{lk_unmeth_stable_std}} and {{lk_unmeth_stable_dense}} in the unmethylated one), and a 21-day regimen at the standard cumulative dose achieves no measurable kill ({{lk_meth_stable_equal}} log$_{10}$ units): the death hazard rises steeply with concentration, so spreading the same dose over more days loses efficacy, and the advantage of the dose-dense arm in the model is entirely its larger cumulative dose. With stable MGMT the two arms therefore differ little in the unmethylated population ({{lk_unmeth_stable_std}} against {{lk_unmeth_stable_dense}} log$_{10}$ units), as in the trial. In the methylated population, where almost no cell is protected, they differ a great deal. The death hazard is already close to saturation at both daily peaks, so halving the dose per day costs less than quadrupling the number of dosing days gains, and the dose-dense arm reaches {{lk_meth_stable_dense}} against {{lk_meth_stable_std}} log$_{10}$ units on cumulative dose alone, with MGMT stable and no consumption at all. RTOG 0525 found no advantage in the methylated subgroup either, so that discrepancy belongs to the model's mapping from dose to kill, and to the exposure it assumes was delivered, rather than to anything about MGMT. Consumption changes that. Because one molecule is spent per lesion repaired, the pool follows the cumulative exposure, which the dose-dense regimen more than doubles: in the methylated population its response deepens to {{lk_meth_consumed_dense}} log$_{10}$ units against {{lk_meth_consumed_std}} for the standard regimen, and in the unmethylated population, where 30% of cells start protected, the same depletion turns a regimen that barely worked into one that controls the population ({{lk_unmeth_consumed_dense}} against {{lk_unmeth_consumed_std}} log$_{10}$ units; final size {{nend_unmeth_consumed_dense}} against {{nend_unmeth_consumed_std}} times the initial size, and {{nend_unmeth_stable_std}} and {{nend_unmeth_stable_dense}} with stable MGMT). Modelled as the suicide enzyme it is, MGMT therefore creates a dose-dense advantage where the model had none: in the unmethylated population, consumption alone separates two regimens that the stable model, like the trial, ranked as equivalent. That is the inference the trial can carry, and read as a bound rather than as a fit it is informative: either temozolomide consumes MGMT in unmethylated tumours far more slowly than this stoichiometry allows, or the benefit of depleting it is offset by something the model leaves out. It cannot be carried over to the methylated population, where the divergence from the trial is already present with MGMT stable and is merely deepened by consumption ({{lk_meth_consumed_dense}} against {{lk_meth_stable_dense}} log$_{10}$ units for the dose-dense arm), so the null there bounds the consumption rate not at all. At the standard cumulative dose the number of dosing days per cycle that minimises the final population is {{days_methylated_stable}} (methylated) and {{days_unmethylated_stable}} (unmethylated) with stable MGMT, and {{days_methylated_consumed}} and {{days_unmethylated_consumed}} when MGMT is consumed (Fig. 2c). These simulations are not fits to the trial, whose end points depend on much that the model omits, dose-limiting toxicity and the dose reductions it forces among it: grade 3 or worse events were far commoner in the dose-dense arm, 53% against 34%, mostly lymphopenia and fatigue [@gilbert2013], while both arms here receive their nominal dose for all six cycles, so the twofold difference in exposure that drives the modelled separation need not be the difference the patients received. What they add is a sharper version of the rationale for dose-dense dosing, and it holds for unmethylated tumours alone: there the benefit that dose-dense dosing is supposed to deliver exists in the model only at consumption rates that the trial's null result appears to exclude. In methylated tumours the modelled advantage comes from cumulative dose rather than from depletion, so its absence in the trial bounds nothing about MGMT and marks instead the limit of a dose response calibrated on the peak concentration. The quantity the argument rests on is the per-lesion molecular rate constant of that consumption, which has not been measured directly, although bulk depletion time-courses have: protracted temozolomide lowers alkyltransferase activity in peripheral blood mononuclear cells by about 63% at 14 days and 73% at 21 days [@tolcher2003], a compressed 1,000 mg/m² schedule takes it to 18% of initial levels while O⁶-methylguanine accumulates in both blood cells and tumour biopsies [@middleton2000], and in orthotopic GBM43 xenografts tumour MGMT activity is depleted by day 6 on both the standard and the 21-day schedule and stays suppressed only on the 21-day one [@robinson2010]. The last of these is the sharpest comparison available, and it makes the disagreement more specific than the trial alone does: the deeper and longer tumour depletion that the dose-dense schedule is supposed to deliver was confirmed in that model, and survival still did not improve. Either the depletion measured in bulk is not the per-cell depletion that matters for killing, or the benefit of depleting MGMT is offset by something the model leaves out.
