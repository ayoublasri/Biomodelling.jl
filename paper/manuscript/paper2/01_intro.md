Cancer cells that survive targeted or cytotoxic therapy frequently do so without a resistance mutation: they occupy a rare, reversible, drug-tolerant expression state that pre-exists the treatment or is induced by it [@sharma2010; @shaffer2017; @hata2016; @oren2021; @franca2024]. Because such a state is reversible, when the drug is given matters as much as how much of it is given, and two lines of evidence on that question point in opposite directions. In melanoma, intermittent BRAF-inhibitor dosing prevented the emergence of resistance in patient-derived xenografts, in which established resistant tumours regressed when the drug was withdrawn [@dasthakur2013]; in the randomised SWOG S1320 trial, continuous dosing gave the longer progression-free survival [@algazi2020]. In glioblastoma, the RTOG 0525 trial compared the standard temozolomide regimen with a dose-dense one designed to deplete the repair protein MGMT, delivering about twice the cumulative dose, and found no survival difference in either methylated or unmethylated tumours [@gilbert2013].

Compartment models of adaptive therapy established the principle that governs the first of these: holidays pay when resistance carries a fitness cost, and tumour turnover sets how large that cost has to be [@zhang2017adaptive; @strobl2021]. Phenotypic-switching theory added that perturbing how long a resistant state is retained can itself decide an outcome [@gunnarsson2020]. What those treatments leave abstract is the resistant compartment: its size and its switching rates are fitted quantities of the model rather than properties of cells that an experiment measures. Lineage-resolved experiments now measure exactly those properties — the correlation of fates between sisters and cousins, the collapse or preservation of clonal barcode diversity, and the timescale of expression fluctuations relative to the cell cycle [@iyer2025; @shaffer2017; @harmange2023; @oren2021] — so a model whose resistant state is a promoter rather than a compartment can be confronted with them.

Here we put the two dosing questions to such a model. Resistance is one slowly switching gene whose protein sets a cell's death hazard, in cells that grow, replicate their genes, divide and partition their molecules, with a dose-schedule layer and a schedule optimiser on top; the framework, its validation against exact stationary laws, and the fit of the resistance-gene model to time-lapse measurements of cisplatin-treated cells are reported in a companion Article. We do not fit these trials. We ask instead what the model has to assume to produce each reported outcome, and what the outcomes therefore bound. For melanoma the answer is three properties of resistant cells, all of them measurable; for glioblastoma it is a molecular rate constant that has not been measured directly, and a place where the model and the trial disagree for a reason that has nothing to do with MGMT.
