title: "Mechanistic simulation of heritable expression states and drug tolerance in growing, dividing single-cell populations"

abstract: |
  Heritable differences in gene expression can decide which cancer cells survive a drug, yet the simulators used to benchmark single-cell methods model neither growth nor division, and stochastic simulators of dividing cells carry no drug layer. Here we present Biomodelling.jl 2.0, in which stochastic reaction kinetics run inside growing, dividing, drug-treated cells. Exact, hybrid and adaptive kernels couple to exponential growth, volume-scaled transcription, gene replication, division with partitioning, dose schedules with state-dependent killing, a lineage record, observation models and likelihood-free inference. The kernels are validated against exact stationary laws, and the population and lineage layers against closed-form solutions for growing, dividing populations in both the single-lineage and the population-snapshot mode. Expression memory, cell-size scaling and cell-cycle-dependent bursting emerge from this physiology rather than being imposed. One resistance gene with slow promoter switching reproduces the lineage and memory signatures of drug tolerance, and supplies the ground truth that benchmarks of memory genes, network inference and perturbation prediction lack: in a dividing population, correlation and tree-based network inference lose most of their accuracy to volume and gene copy number, and regressing on the cell-cycle covariates that the simulation supplies recovers most of it. Fitted to time-lapse measurements of cisplatin-treated U2OS cells, the model reproduces their dose-invariance of single-cell timing, but six parameters fitted to six fate fractions leave the memory of the resistant state undetermined, a cycle-gated hazard fits as well, and the fit does not reach the precision of the measurement. We report that non-identifiability as a result: it is what a mechanistic forward model can expose and a phenomenological fit cannot.

## Author summary

Two cancer cells with the same genome, given the same drug, can meet opposite fates. Part of that difference can come from which genes each cell happens to be expressing strongly when treatment begins, and cells pass those states to their daughters when they divide. The simulators used to test single-cell analysis methods model neither growth nor division, so they cannot produce such inherited states; those that do model dividing cells carry no drug.

We built Biomodelling.jl 2.0, in which chemical reactions play out inside cells that grow, copy their genes, divide and meet drugs on realistic schedules. Expression memory, cell-size effects and cell-cycle dependence emerge from that physiology rather than being assumed. We checked it against cases whose answers are known exactly in mathematics, used it to measure how much accuracy methods for reconstructing gene networks lose when cells grow and divide, and fitted it to published time-lapse recordings of cells under chemotherapy.

That last exercise is where a mechanistic model earns its keep. The recordings can be fitted, but they do not pin the model down: how long cells remember their state, and whether they were spared because of what they inherited or because of where they were in the cell cycle, are both left open. A model that names its mechanisms can say so; one fitted to the same numbers without them cannot.
