abstract: |
  Heritable differences in gene expression can decide which cancer cells survive a drug, yet the simulators used to benchmark single-cell methods model neither growth nor division, and stochastic simulators of dividing cells carry no drug layer. Here we present Biomodelling.jl 2.0, in which stochastic reaction kinetics run inside growing, dividing, drug-treated cells. Exact, hybrid and adaptive kernels couple to exponential growth, volume-scaled transcription, gene replication, division with partitioning, dose schedules with state-dependent killing, a lineage record, observation models and likelihood-free inference. Expression memory, cell-size scaling and cell-cycle-dependent bursting emerge from this physiology rather than being imposed. One resistance gene with slow promoter switching reproduces the lineage and memory signatures of drug tolerance, and the framework supplies the ground truth that benchmarks of memory genes, network inference and perturbation prediction lack. Calibrated to time-lapse measurements of cisplatin-treated U2OS cells, the model reproduces their dose-invariance of single-cell timing and matches the kin fate correlations reported for HCT116 cells in the same study. That match is a consistency check across cell lines rather than an independent validation, because the memory timescale was taken from those correlations as a prior; six parameters fitted to six fate fractions leave it undetermined, and a cycle-gated hazard fits as well. The schedule optimiser reproduces the opposite rankings of intermittent dosing reported in a melanoma trial and in xenografts, the xenograft ranking as a lower tumour burden at the end of the 60-week simulation rather than as a longer time to loss of control, on which all three schedules are censored, and shows the ranking to turn on measurable properties of resistant cells: growth under drug, fitness without it, and whether the drug stabilises the resistant state.

## Author summary

Two cancer cells with the same genome, given the same drug, can meet opposite fates. Part of that difference can come from which genes each cell happens to be expressing strongly when treatment begins, and cells pass those states to their daughters when they divide. The simulators used to test single-cell analysis methods model neither growth nor division, so they cannot produce such inherited states; those that do model dividing cells carry no drug.

We built Biomodelling.jl 2.0, in which chemical reactions play out inside cells that grow, copy their genes, divide and meet drugs on realistic schedules. Expression memory, cell-size effects and cell-cycle dependence emerge from that physiology rather than being assumed. We checked it against cases whose answers are known exactly in mathematics, fitted it to published time-lapse recordings of cells under chemotherapy, and asked it to predict a concentration it had not seen.

We then put a clinical question to it: should a drug be given continuously, or with breaks? The answer turns on three measurable properties of resistant cells: how fast they grow under the drug, how they fare without it, and whether the drug itself holds the resistant state in place.
